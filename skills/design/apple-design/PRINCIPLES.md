# Apple's design principles and process

Apple frames design as serving four human needs: safety and predictability, understanding, achievement, and joy. Use the eight principles below as the names you reason with when you judge a design.

## The eight principles

1. **Purpose.** Decide what not to build. Each feature costs the user time, attention and trust, so spend those only where they pay back.
2. **Agency.** Keep people in control. Offer choices, not one forced path. Make slips easy to undo. Ask for confirmation only before a destructive action that cannot be undone, because frequent confirmations teach people to click through.
3. **Responsibility.** Act in the interest of the user. Ask for private data at the moment of need, only for what the feature needs, and say why. Anticipate misuse and harm, most of all with AI: a recipe app that knows an allergy does not suggest that ingredient. Add previews, confirmations and disclaimers. Cut a feature whose risk is larger than its value.
4. **Familiarity.** Build on what people know. Pick a metaphor that is neither too literal nor too abstract, and follow its physics. Things that look the same behave the same and sit in the same place, so people can predict the result. Break a familiar pattern only when a test shows the new one is better.
5. **Flexibility.** Design for different contexts, devices and abilities. A phone suits quick touch, and a desktop suits deep work with a precise pointer. Design for a range of age, language, expertise and access needs. When no single layout fits, let people rearrange controls and hide what they do not use.
6. **Simplicity, not minimalism.** Remove the unnecessary so the core purpose is clear. A design that hides everything in one place looks minimal and is not simple. Use plain words and fewer steps. Use order, spacing and contrast so the most important thing is the most obvious. Added context can simplify: a video scrubber that shows the time left. Show the common path first and advanced options one level down.
7. **Craft.** Attention to detail builds trust. Each spacing, timing and alignment value is a choice you can defend. Scroll that jitters, icons out of line, and a layout that breaks on rotation read as carelessness. Keep the design current as features and hardware change.
8. **Delight.** Delight is the result of the other seven, not decoration added at the end. Decide the emotion the product gives (calm, confident, excited) and support it in each decision.

## Rules that serve the principles

- **Feedback has four kinds:** status, completion, warning and error. Confirm actions that matter, show ongoing status, warn before a problem, and validate inline, not on submit.
- **Wayfinding:** each screen answers four questions. Where am I? Where can I go? What is there? How do I get out?
- **Grouping and mapping:** proximity implies relation. Put a control near what it affects, and arrange controls to mirror what they change. A control that needs a label to explain it has a weak mapping.
- **Specific labels:** name a navigation item for its contents ("Progress", "Library"), not for a vague group ("Home"). A specific label lets the user predict the screen. `copy` covers label wording.

## Process

- **Prototype the interaction.** You find the interface by building it and using it. A working demo also sets a concrete bar for the final build. The engineering skill `prototype` covers how.
- **Design interaction and visuals together.** Motion is part of the design from the start, not a layer added after the pixels.
- **Test with real people in real context.**
- **Review motion with fresh eyes.** Play it in slow motion or frame by frame to find what full speed hides.
